Routine Maintenance

Microsoft, the FAA, the banks and the NSA all fell over in the one week. Nobody has shown an attack. Here is the cause first, and the detail after.

Eriu Technology  ·  17 November 2026  ·  Week-in-review briefing
The cause, first
The cause

Ordinary engineering failures. A software defect that sat unnoticed for months. A telecom fault that flooded an air traffic network. A bug in a bank's on-premise hardware. A maintenance window that went sideways. No cyberattack has been shown for any of them.

That is the short version. The longer one is worth having, because the week looked like a plot and read like a spreadsheet of excuses.

On Friday I wrote up the Microsoft outage and what it takes to run your own email: Recover Control of Your Email. By Tuesday the same week, the people whose whole job is reading other people's messages could not reach their own. Here is the week in order, with the stated cause up front each time.

02The Outlook zero-day (not an outage)

Cause: a flaw that attackers were already using. Microsoft's Patch Tuesday on 10 November fixed 1,094 vulnerabilities. One of them, CVE-2026-124194, is an Outlook spoofing flaw rated moderate (CVSS 6.5) that Microsoft confirms is exploited. A malicious Office file can make Outlook reach out to an attacker, which can leak the user's NTLMv2 password hash.

The detail that catches people out: the fix comes through Office's own updates, so installing the Windows cumulative update does not necessarily close it. A critical Windows DHCP Server flaw (CVE-2026-88243, CVSS 9.8) was patched the same day. At the time of reporting it was not listed as exploited and had no public proof of concept.

Neither of these caused any outage that followed. They are a separate story that happened to land the same week. Patch both.

03Microsoft 365: a day and a half

Cause: a latent defect. A Microsoft executive explained on Monday 16 November that a software defect, unnoticed on its servers for months, left them overwhelmed and unresponsive under certain load. The defect came from a Windows fix prepared for Microsoft 365 networking. Extra Teams traffic and lost server capacity piled on top.

The outage hit Outlook, Teams, Copilot and SharePoint in more than 70 countries. By Microsoft's own timestamps it ran from about 16:17 GMT on Thursday 12 November to early Saturday morning, so roughly a day and a half. Sources range from 24 to 36 hours depending on how you count. Microsoft said it was “not related to cyber or AI/agents”, paused deployments and config changes while it reviewed, and promised a full post-incident report.

04The FAA: 4 hours 21 minutes

Cause: legacy equipment buckling under a flood of traffic. The FAA says its legacy equipment became unstable when data flooded its FTI network at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every 4 to 5 seconds. That disrupted aircraft communications and radar feeds. Officials said they traced the telecom problem to its origin, took that location offline, and the system recovered.

The ground stop ran from 20:54 GMT (3:54 pm EST) to 01:15 GMT (8:15 pm EST). Communications problems affected all of the en-route centres. The Transportation Secretary said “it wasn’t a foreign actor”.

The money followed the same day. Congress approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control upgrades in July 2025. Late on the Friday, the administration raised its funding request to $50 billion, up from $30.1 billion earlier that day. The FAA has still not said what started the flood.

05The banks: Monday morning

Cause: a software bug in on-premise hardware. That was Chase's explanation. It said there was no evidence of a cyber cause, that customers still had access to their accounts and funds, and that where it was party to a transaction with other banks, those banks may have been affected, but this was not a systemic issue.

Downdetector logged nearly 5,000 Chase reports and 800-plus for Wells Fargo, with Bank of America and U.S. Bank also affected. Chase was back to normal by Monday evening.

06The NSA: Tuesday

Cause given: scheduled routine maintenance. The agency’s classified internal network, NSANet, went offline. Staff arrivals were staggered at Fort Meade, and officials told reporters there was no evidence of a cyberattack so far. Backup systems were reported to be keeping urgent intelligence flowing to the President. NSANet is the internal classified messaging network, so strictly it is not email, but it is the same job.

A bipartisan group of lawmakers is pushing for briefings. One senator counted “five major outages in less than a week”.

07What we know, what we don’t, and what is opinion

  • KnownFour different organisations gave four different technical explanations, and officials say there is no sign of a coordinated attack.
  • UnknownWhat started the flood on the FAA network. Whether any of the outages are connected. What Microsoft’s final report says. What actually went wrong at the NSA beyond one sentence.
  • OpinionAgeing kit, big promises, big contracts and bigger asks: I would like to see where the money goes. I have no internal knowledge, and nothing in the reporting proves anything improper.

08What it means for your business

  • Patch Office this week, not just Windows. The exploited Outlook flaw is fixed through Office updates.
  • Have a plan B for email. A shared inbox is a single point of failure. Recover Control of Your Email shows what a fallback looks like.
  • Write the outage plan on paper. If email and Teams are both down, who phones whom, and on which number?
  • Expect phishing in the gap. “Your mailbox is suspended, click to verify” arrives the moment a service wobbles. Check the provider’s status page, not the email.
  • Do not reset passwords or change DNS in a panic. Find out whether it is you or them first.
  • List what you depend on. Five services that would stop your business if they stopped. Know who to ring for each.
