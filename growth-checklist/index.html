    Tue 10 Nov - Plug & Prey
    Part of one of my jobs was collecting USB sticks.

Walking between the buildings of the German Bundestag, I'd pick up six or seven a day: window ledges, car parks, down the side of chairs. Every one went in the bin, never into a laptop. Nobody loses that many sticks by accident in a parliament. Somebody's leaving them.

The attack is called BadUSB. The stick tells your computer it's a keyboard, and computers trust keyboards completely, so it can type whatever it likes before you've blinked. No file, no link, nothing for the scanner to find.

Found one? Don't plug it in. Hand it to whoever looks after security, or bin it.

The plain-English briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/plug-and-prey/

    Tue 17 Nov - Free Wi-Fi, Dear Price
    Free Wi-Fi isn't free. It's just paid for differently.

Sit in any café or airport lounge and your phone offers you a dozen networks. Anyone with a laptop can broadcast one called "Airport_Free_WiFi" and watch what people do next. In 2024 Australian police charged a man over exactly that: fake Wi-Fi networks at airports that harvested people's logins.

Three habits that cost nothing:
1. Treat public Wi-Fi as a stranger's network, because it is.
2. Don't log in to anything important on it without a VPN.
3. If a login page appears out of nowhere asking for your email and password, close it.

The full briefing, in plain English: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/free-wifi/

#CyberSecurity #SmallBusiness #Ireland
    Tue 24 Nov - Wire & Deceit
    A supplier emails to say their bank details have changed. You update them. Three weeks later they ask why you haven't paid.

That's invoice-redirection fraud, and it's the main email scam in the figures. FraudSMART, run by BPFI, reported that Irish SMEs lost almost €10 million to email-related scams in 2023, about €12,000 each time on average, and most cases were this exact trick.

The fix isn't clever software. It's a rule: any change of bank details gets confirmed by phone, on a number you already had, before any money moves.

Thirty seconds on the phone beats a five-figure wire you'll never see again. The briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/wire-and-deceit/

#FraudAwareness #SmallBusiness #Ireland
    Tue 1 Dec - The Phish & Chips
    Phishing isn't about being gullible. It's about being busy.

It's a Tuesday, you're twelve emails deep, and one says your Microsoft password expires today. The page looks right, the logo's right, you type your password and move on. So does the person now reading your mailbox.

Three habits help: never log in through a link in a message (open the app or type the address yourself); be suspicious of anything that rushes you, like "expires today" or "parcel held"; and have two-step login on, so a stolen password isn't enough.

The usual disguises, in plain English: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/the-phish-and-chips/

#Phishing #CyberSecurity #SmallBusiness
    Tue 8 Dec - Held to Ransom
    The worst part of ransomware isn't the ransom note. It's finding out your backup doesn't work.

You come in on a Monday and every file is gibberish. You're grand, you think, there's a backup. Then you go to restore it and find it stopped running in March, or it's encrypted too, or nobody ever tried bringing it back.

Two rules: keep a backup copy offline or immutable, so ransomware can't reach it, and test a restore on a schedule. A backup you haven't restored is a hope, not a backup.

The full briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/held-to-ransom/

#Ransomware #Backup #SmallBusiness
    Tue 15 Dec - Keys to the Kingdom
    The problem was never your password's strength. It's the spare key under every mat.

One site you joined in 2015 gets breached. Your email and password end up on a list, and bots try that pair on your bank, your email and your work login. It only has to fit one lock.

What saves you: a password manager, a unique password for every site, and two-step login, starting with your email, because email is the key that resets everything else.

If you do one thing today, switch on MFA for your email. More here: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/keys-to-the-kingdom/

#Passwords #MFA #CyberSecurity
    Tue 22 Dec - The Voice on the Phone
    We've learned to doubt a strange email. We haven't learned to doubt a familiar voice.

Voice-cloning tools can copy someone's voice from a short clip: a podcast, a webinar, a video on the company website. Add urgency and secrecy, and "a call from the boss asking for a quick transfer" becomes very hard to question in the moment.

The fix is dull and it works: hang up, and ring the person back on a number you already had. Agree a safe word for genuine emergencies. A real boss will thank you for checking.

The briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/the-voice-on-the-phone/

#Deepfake #FraudAwareness #SmallBusiness
    Tue 29 Dec - The Leaving Do
    Someone leaves on good terms. Cake, a card, handshakes, desk cleared by Friday.

Six months on, their email still forwards, their VPN still connects, and the tool nobody remembers they set up is still billing your card. Every one of those is an unlocked door with nobody watching it.

Offboarding is a security job, not just an HR one. Keep a leaver checklist: email and forwarding, VPN and remote access, shared logins, apps and tools, keys and tokens, and the phone.

    Tue 5 Jan - Cloudy with a Chance of Breach
    Your business lives in the cloud, so the lock on the whole business is one login.

An attacker doesn't need to breach Microsoft. They just need to be you: a phished password, or an "approve this app to continue" screen clicked through without reading. That second trick gives a dodgy app standing access to your mail and files, and two-step login doesn't stop it. Then a hidden rule quietly forwards your mail to them.

Worth a quick check: the mail rules on your account, and the apps connected to it.

More in the briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/cloudy-with-a-chance-of-breach/

    Tue 12 Jan - Shadow IT
    Nobody meant any harm.

Someone in accounts needed to share a big file, so they opened a free account on a site they'd heard of. Someone in sales wired the CRM into a handy automation tool. Now bits of your business live in apps you've never heard of, secured by passwords you don't control. AI chatbots count too.

You can't protect what you can't see. Start by asking people what they actually use, with no blame attached.

#ShadowIT #DataProtection #SmallBusiness
    Tue 19 Jan - Patch or Perish
    "Later" is the most dangerous button on your computer.

When a software maker releases a security update, they also publish what it fixes. Attackers read the same note and go looking for everyone who hasn't applied it yet. The patch and the attack arrive together.

An unpatched system isn't a secret weakness. It's a published one. Turn on automatic updates, and don't forget the quiet corners: browsers, routers, firewalls and old kit nobody retired.

The briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/patch-or-perish/

#Patching #CyberSecurity #SmallBusiness
    Tue 26 Jan - The Lost Laptop
    Twenty minutes in the boot. One broken window. One missing bag.

If the laptop was encrypted and locked, you've lost a few hundred quid of hardware and an afternoon. If it wasn't, you've lost every file on it, every saved password in the browser, and possibly a reportable data breach.

The difference is a setting you turn on once: BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac. Check yours today.

The briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/the-lost-laptop/

#DataProtection #Encryption #SmallBusiness
    Tue 2 Feb - The Smart Office
    You locked down the laptops and forgot the kettle has Wi-Fi.

Printers, cameras, routers, the smart TV in the meeting room: each one is a small computer on your network, often with the default password still set and software last updated the day it was made. Attackers love them because nobody is looking.

Change the defaults, update them, and keep a list of what's plugged in. If you can't say what's on your network, you can't secure it.

The briefing: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/the-smart-office/

    Tue 9 Feb - GDPR for the Terrified
    GDPR tends to land like a solicitor's letter: vaguely threatening, probably expensive, best not thought about.

For an ordinary small business it's mostly common sense written down. Know what personal data you hold and where it lives. Keep only what you need, for only as long as you need it. Keep it secure. And be ready if someone asks to see or delete theirs.

Do those four and you're most of the way there. The plain-English version: https://lolo737.github.io/eriu-security-blog/gdpr-for-the-terrified/

    Three questions for a school bursar
    Three questions every school bursar should be able to answer about their IT, without phoning anyone:

1. If a teacher deleted last Tuesday's shared folder today, who can get it back, and how long would it take?
2. Who has administrator access to your systems, and would you know if that list changed?

If the honest answer to any of them is "I'm not sure", that's not a failing. It's a to-do. Schools hold sensitive information about children and families, and run on tight budgets and term-time deadlines, so predictable and boring is the goal.

    It's nine o'clock on a Sunday night and the owner of a small firm is on the sofa with a laptop, trying to work out why the shared drive has stopped letting anyone in. He isn't an IT person. He's a builder, or a bursar, or a bookkeeper. Somewhere along the way he became the IT department anyway, the way the person who knows where the fuse box is becomes the electrician.

I'd say that scene is in more small organisations than anyone admits. Nobody chose it. It happened one favour at a time.

## What IT is actually for

Good IT is boring. That's the whole job. The aim isn't clever technology. It's a Monday morning where everyone logs in, the files are where they were on Friday, and nobody has to think about any of it.

I learned that in two places where boring was the goal. I worked in IT security at the German Bundestag, and later in IT support at Berlin Brandenburg Airport. In both, a quiet day meant everything was working.

What I took from them is that serious trouble is rarely dramatic. It's small things nobody had time to look at: a backup that stopped running months ago, an account that outlived the person who left, an update postponed for the thirtieth time. Then, one day, the small thing isn't small.

## Five questions to ask about your own IT

You don't need to be technical to ask these. If the answer to any of them is "I'm not sure", that's not a failing. It's a to-do.

1. If someone deleted last Tuesday's shared folder today, who could get it back, and how long would it take? Ask for a restore test, not a promise. A backup nobody has restored is a hope, not a backup.
2. Who has administrator access, and would you know if that list changed?
3. When someone leaves, how fast does their access go? Email forwarding, remote access, shared logins and the tool nobody remembers signing up for all outlive the leaving do.
4. Are your devices and software kept up to date, and by whom? When a maker releases a security fix, they publish what it fixes. Attackers read the same notes.
5. If something breaks at nine on a Monday morning, who do you ring, and do they already know your setup?

## What good looks like

Not a heroic person. A routine. Someone who knows what you have, keeps it up to date, tests the backups, closes accounts when people leave, and tells you in plain English when something needs money spending on it.

A fixed monthly price helps more than you'd think, because nobody hesitates to ring someone when the call isn't ticking a meter.

## Three things you can do this week

None of these needs a consultant.

1. Switch on multi-factor authentication for your email. Email is the key that resets every other account you own.
2. Pick one backup and restore a single file from it. If you can't, you've found out on a quiet day.
3. Write down everyone who has left in the last two years, and check that their accounts are closed.

## The scam that keeps working

Most of what catches small organisations isn't sophisticated. The big one in Ireland is the supplier who "changes their bank details". FraudSMART, run by the Banking & Payments Federation Ireland, reported that Irish SMEs lost almost €10 million to email-related scams in 2023, and most cases were this exact trick. The defence is a phone call: confirm any change of bank details on a number you already had, before any money moves.

Watch out: the rules on cold email

Marketing by email in Ireland is covered by GDPR and the ePrivacy Regulations (S.I. 336 of 2011). The Data Protection Commission's guidance says when consent is needed, what the exception for existing customers allows, and how people can object. I couldn't confirm from the DPC page how business addresses are treated, and I'm not a lawyer, so check before you build a list.

The safe way to start: email people who've asked to hear from you or you've met (for example at Chamber networking), use LinkedIn messages and phone calls for first contact, keep the list small, record where each address came from, and put who you are and an easy opt-out in every email.
