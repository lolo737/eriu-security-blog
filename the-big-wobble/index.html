The Big Wobble

What actually happened to the CIA, FBI, NSA, FinCEN and friends in November 2026, what caused each one, and what it means for your office.

Eriu Technology  ·  28 November 2026
One bad week, lined up

You know the way a bad week in the office looks like a dozen unrelated problems? The printer's down, somebody's locked out, the accounts package is crawling, and then someone lines the dates up on a whiteboard and it turns out they're all connected. Or, as it sometimes turns out, they're not, and you've spent a day blaming the wrong thing.

That was the American government's fortnight. The CIA, the FBI, the Air Force, the Treasury and the Department of Agriculture all had incidents land within days of each other, and the NSA's network fell over for good measure. The headlines read like a thriller. The detail reads more like an IT department having a very long month.

So here are all nine, one at a time, with what's confirmed, what's only reported, and what actually caused it. The lesson is about your office, not theirs: the unglamorous stuff, patching, passwords and forgotten side doors, is where the damage starts.

What it actually is

There isn't one big attack here. There's a cluster: some confirmed intrusions, some intrusions reported by anonymous sources, and one outage that officials say was a software update gone sideways. Reporters, officials and even a few of the agencies disagree on the details, and most of it is still under investigation. Treat everything below as the picture on 28 November 2026, not the final word.

The nine, one by one

The Open-Source Intelligence System isn't an agency in its own right. It's the shared system at the heart of the CIA story, so it gets its own entry. Everything here is sourced from news reports; where something is unconfirmed, it says so.

1. US Air Force

Reuters reported on 27 November that hackers got into Cloud One, the Air Force's flagship cloud platform. The intrusion was reportedly found within the past month during an upgrade, and it disrupted application migrations and software updates. One source told Reuters the attackers used an unidentified software vulnerability, which means a flaw nobody had fixed (or perhaps even named). Who did it, and what they saw, hasn't been made public. Sensitive operational and logistics information could have been exposed.

2. Central Intelligence Agency

On 24 November the New York Times reported that attackers had broken into an unclassified CIA system used to analyse public and commercially available information. No classified systems were affected, and a source said the matter has been resolved. A person familiar with the case said an AI-driven hacking framework called ARTEX was believed to have been used. Nobody has said who was behind it or how they got in, and the CIA declined to comment.

3. Federal Bureau of Investigation

CNN reported around 20 November that the FBI was investigating an apparent breach of the National Crime Information Center, the database police across the US use to check warrants, missing persons, stolen vehicles and firearms. It was the second reported FBI incident in two months. The first, in September, hit a jobs portal and was claimed by a group called ShinyHunters, who said they'd taken staff data. For the NCIC, the FBI is still working out how the intruders got in and what, if anything, was taken. A retired agent warned that altered records could put officers at risk at traffic stops, but that is a concern, not something reported to have happened.

4. Office of the Director of National Intelligence

ODNI shares the CIA's open-source system and was named alongside it. An ODNI official said there were no disruptions to critical capabilities or classified systems. The cause is the same as the CIA case: unknown entry route, suspected AI tooling, no confirmed culprit.

5. National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency

The Pentagon's mapping and imagery agency was reported by the New York Times as 'concurrently affected'. NGA didn't respond to requests for comment, so its involvement rests on that one report and hasn't been confirmed by the agency itself.

6. The open-source intelligence system (OSINT)

This is the system, not a department. Analysts use it to study foreign governments and militaries from public and commercially available material, so there's nothing classified in it. The risk is subtler: if someone's been inside, they may be able to see what analysts search for and track, which is a map of what US intelligence cares about and where it's looking thin. The same ARTEX tool was reportedly used in a separate low-impact intrusion at the Defense Department's Cyber Defense Command, though nobody has linked the two.

7. US Department of Agriculture

USDA's own security operations centre uncovered a security incident, according to an internal email dated 16 November. Many applications went offline over several days, including the financial tools used to pay farmers and the screens showing loan and aid status. The department reset every database password in every environment, and because every application had to be reset to match, that is what caused the outages. Staff reportedly felt poorly informed. The cause of the intrusion, the culprit, and whether any data was taken have not been reported.

8. Treasury: FinCEN

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network announced on 24 November that someone had gained unauthorised access to 'a limited subset' of its systems. It took some systems offline as a precaution, kept its Bank Secrecy Act filing portal running, and extended deadlines for affected filers. Investigators currently see no evidence that the main suspicious-activity reports database or the company-ownership database were breached. FinCEN hasn't said how the intruder got in, and no group has claimed it.

9. National Security Agency

This one wasn't a hack. On 17 November the NSA's internal classified network went down, staff were told to come in at staggered times, and the agency said it was 'temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance'. The Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, said it came from patching software after a PHP vulnerability was found the month before, and that some systems didn't come back up as expected. Officials said there was no evidence of a cyberattack. The NSA stayed operational throughout, but the episode is still a good example of how a fix can break things.

01How it works

  1. A flaw turns upSometimes in software you run, sometimes in a portal or platform nobody on your team owns.
  2. Machines find it firstAI-driven tools (ARTEX is the one named in reports) can scan and try entry points far quicker than a human team.
  3. They come in the side doorAn unclassified system, a contractor platform, a cloud upgrade. Not the vault.
  4. You scrambleSystems come offline, every password gets reset, and the clean-up hurts almost as much as the intruder.

→ Even when no secrets leave the building, the outage, the doubt and the bill all arrive.

Where it hits

The same four weak spots turn up in every office, big or small:

  • Unpatched software — A known flaw, left open a few weeks too long.
  • Third-party portals — The side door that nobody in-house owns.
  • Rushed patching — The fix that knocks the lights out.
  • Shared platforms — One system, several organisations' worth of exposure.
Patch fast, but patch with a safety net

Apply security updates quickly, because attackers' tools get faster every month. But test them on one machine first, take a backup or snapshot beforehand, and know how to roll back. The NSA shows that a rushed patch can cause an outage as surely as an attacker can.

02Why it's dangerous

  • Speed has flipped. Automated tools shrink the window between a flaw being found and being used.
  • The boring system is the target. 'Unclassified' or 'low value' systems often reveal more than you'd think, such as what you're watching and where you're thin.
  • Resets hurt too. Changing every password at once is the right instinct and still takes services down.
  • Not knowing is its own cost. Staff left in the dark, and customers left guessing, do real damage.

03How you stop it

  • Patch on a schedule, critical fixes within days, and test before you roll out to everyone.
  • Keep a rollback plan and a recent backup before any major update.
  • Inventory your third parties. Know every portal, supplier platform and cloud tool that touches your data, and who owns each.
  • Turn on MFA everywhere and keep an up-to-date list of admin accounts, so a reset is a plan and not a panic.
  • Write the incident plan before you need it: who decides, who tells staff, who tells customers.
